--- layout: post title: "How mixed are Scottish wildcats? Fitting demographic models to a joint site frequency spectrum" date: 2026-09-15 summary: "Fitting 2D demographic models in dadi to a Scottish wildcat / domestic cat joint SFS." ---

The Scottish wildcat is hanging on by a thread. There are only a few hundred left, and the bigger threat to them now isn't habitat or persecution but domestic cats: the two interbreed freely, and recent work has shown that the wild population has been steadily absorbing domestic ancestry. How much, and over what timescale, is a question you can try to answer from the genomes themselves.

Over the summer I worked on this with Mark Beaumont, Dennis Prangle and Grace Yan. The idea was to fit a few competing models of the two populations' shared history to sequence data from 16 wild-caught Scottish wildcats and 6 domestic cats, ask which model fits best, and be honest about which of its parameters the data actually pin down. This post is the short version. The full report and the code are both available.

The data: a joint site frequency spectrum

Demographic inference from a site frequency spectrum (SFS) throws away almost everything about the genome and keeps one thing: for each variable site, how many copies of each allele are in the sample. A joint SFS does this for two populations at once, so each site becomes a pair of counts, (copies in the wildcats, copies in the domestics), and the spectrum is a 2D histogram of those pairs.

Ours is folded because we have no outgroup, so we can't tell which allele is ancestral. Instead of counting derived alleles, you count the minor allele, which folds the histogram over its diagonal. That costs some information (in particular some of the asymmetry between the populations), but it avoids guessing.

The shape of this histogram is what carries the history. Lots of rare variants means recent expansion; a bulge at intermediate frequencies means a bottleneck; mass along the diagonal means shared variation, and so on. A demographic model predicts an expected spectrum, and you fit it by comparing the prediction to the observed one.

One decision here mattered more than I expected. The Scottish sample also included ten captive-bred animals, and I'd initially planned to pool them with the wild-caught ones. But the captive cats are a small, closed breeding population with about 20% lower diversity, and a spectrum built from a structured population mimics a recent size change that never happened. Pooling shifted 26% more mass into the middle of the spectrum. So all the fits use the wild-caught animals only.

Three models

All three share a topology: one ancestral population splits into a wildcat branch and a domestic branch, and the two then exchange migrants up to the present. They differ in how much structure is imposed on top.

The interesting comparison is between sec_contact, whose six parameters correspond to nothing in particular, and basic, which Mark specified with two named events in mind: the size change in the domestic branch represents domestication, and the size change in the wildcat branch represents Britain being cut off from the continent when the land bridge flooded. If the labelled model fits better, that's weak evidence the labels mean something.

What dadi does with them

dadi takes a model and solves a diffusion approximation to get the expected SFS under it, then computes a Poisson likelihood of the observed spectrum given that expectation, and optimises over the parameters. The fitting itself is routine: four rounds of fifty restarts each on the university's HPC, narrowing the search each time.

The part that took the most care is that dadi's likelihood is a composite likelihood. It treats every site as independent, which they aren't, because nearby sites are linked. The result is that the likelihood is far sharper than it should be, and every standard tool built on it (AIC, confidence intervals) is over-confident. For model selection I used the composite-likelihood AIC, which replaces the parameter count with an effective count estimated from block-bootstrap replicates of the genome. For basic, a 12-parameter model, the effective count came out at 293. That's roughly how much the naive likelihood overstates what the data say.

What came out

sec_contact lost decisively, by over 800 CLAIC units, and you can see why in the residuals: it systematically misses the bins where both populations have low allele counts. basic and growth both fit well and are hard to tell apart. growth technically scored better, but its fit ran into a parameter bound rather than finding a maximum, which means the statistic isn't defined for it. So basic is the result I report.

Under basic:

  • The wildcat and domestic lineages split around 485,000 years ago, though the interval on that runs from 190,000 to 1.3 million.
  • The domestic branch changed size around 10,600 years ago, which lines up nicely with the earliest cats found alongside humans on Cyprus. But the interval is 3,200 to 35,000 years, so treating it as a date would be generous.
  • The wildcat branch changed size about 670 years ago. This is the awkward one: the land bridge flooded around 8,200 years ago, and the 19th-century persecution is more recent. The model has a single break point where the real history has several, so it's settled on a compromise matching none of them. The land-bridge label doesn't survive.
  • Recent gene flow runs strongly from domestic cats into wildcats, and this holds across both good models even though they disagree about almost everything ancient. Compounded over the recent epoch, the fitted rate gives roughly 14% domestic ancestry, at the low end of the 11–74% measured directly in modern individuals.

The part I'd want a reader to take away

Only four of the twelve parameters are determined to within a factor of two, and they're all recent: the current wildcat size, the time of its change, and the two recent migration rates. Everything deeper is known only to within a factor of four to eleven. That's partly the data (two chromosomes is not much sequence) and partly a fundamental limit: the error in SFS-based inference shrinks only logarithmically with the number of individuals but polynomially with the amount of sequence, so adding cats wouldn't have helped much.

More importantly, the interval widths aren't independent of each other.

The ancestral sizes and the split time aren't three separately uncertain quantities. They lie along a single ridge in the likelihood: make the ancestral populations bigger and the split older while turning down θ and early gene flow, and the fit barely changes. Even the "well determined" recent parameters have this problem in miniature: the recent wildcat epoch length and the migration rate into it correlate at 0.987, so what the spectrum really constrains is their product, roughly the cumulative amount of introgression, and not either one on its own.

That last point is the one I find most interesting, because it lines up with the literature in an unexpected way. Earlier work estimated a much higher migration rate over a much shorter period, and the two histories reach a similar cumulative ancestry. My hypothesis is that the SFS pins down the total quite well and the rate and duration hardly at all.

Why this was worth doing

The real aim was to build a baseline. My MSc dissertation will use neural simulation-based inference for demographic modelling, and to know whether that's working I need something to compare against. What this project taught me is that the comparison can't just be a table of interval widths. A method could reproduce every width in that table while placing its uncertainty along a completely different ridge. The correlation structure is part of the answer, and the honest uncertainty is both wider and more structured than any single table shows.

Things I'd do next, roughly in order: refit the plain isolation-with-migration model that was dropped early on, since without it the comparison lacks its natural baseline; add sequence rather than animals; and try to polarise the spectrum, since folding discards exactly the asymmetry the worst-determined parameters describe.
